The Danelaw Way

A ride along the waterways and roads of the Danelaw, the part of England under Danish rule from the late 9th century: down the Trent past the Great Army's winter camps, along the Fossdyke into Lincoln, up Ermine Street to the Humber, then the Ouse to Viking York. It is England's answer to Denmark's DGI Hærvejsløbet, which follows the old Army Road through Jutland on routes of up to 300 km. This one is about as long and far flatter.

Like Hærvejsløbet's set of routes, the Way splits into shorter rides that each start and finish at a railway town.

Stages

Five stages of 46 to 78 km. The bar shows each stage's riding conditions; signed routes are the National Cycle Network and other waymarked cycle routes it follows.

Gaps to fix

Stretches of 300 m or more where the route has to use an A road, with the police-reported cycle collisions on each (2021–25). These are where a protected path or a crossing would make the Way safe for everyone, not just confident riders.

The Danelaw along the route

In order from Derby. Select a place to see it on the map.

Route is a proposal, routed with BRouter's touring profile (which prefers cycle routes and quiet roads) through waypoints that keep it by the Trent, the Fossdyke, Ermine Street, the Humber and the Ouse. It needs riding and checking on the ground, especially farm tracks and towpaths after wet weather.
Riding conditions are measured every 25 m against OpenStreetMap: traffic-free is within 12 m of a cycleway or a path designated for bikes; quiet road a residential, unclassified or service road; track or lane no named road or cycle path within 12 m (unnamed lanes, farm tracks, towpaths); B or C road a secondary or tertiary road; A road a primary or trunk road. Collisions are police-reported injury collisions involving a bicycle within 30 m of the route (DfT STATS19, 2021–25). Height comes from BRouter's elevation data.
History is summarised from standard accounts of the Viking Age in England (the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle and recent archaeology at Repton and Torksey). Place-name meanings are traditional derivations. Method and sources on GitHub.
