Danelaw Cycle Network

A network of flat, safe cycle routes along the rivers of the Danelaw, the part of England under Danish rule from the late 9th century. Its spine, the Danelaw Way, runs from the Great Army's camp at Repton down the Trent past Torksey and Gainsborough, across to Howden and up the Ouse to Viking York and the battlefield of 1066 at Stamford Bridge. Around it, shorter routes bring in Leicester, Leeds, Sheffield, Rotherham, Doncaster, Hull, Bridlington, Northallerton, Middlesbrough, Lincoln, Grimsby, Stamford and the Lincolnshire coast: some for getting to work, some worth riding for their own sake. They follow the rivers rather than wandering between villages, and show where a proper traffic-free path has to be built so nobody rides beside a busy road. England's answer to Denmark's DGI Hærvejsløbet, and far flatter.

What these routes are for

The rest of CycleStats looks for the places where cycling infrastructure is most likely to work. These routes bring those findings together: each one is tested against three things.

They are meant for a wider set of people than keen cyclists. Some are for getting to work: they join towns by the most direct flat line, with no scenic detours, because a commute that is longer than it needs to be does not get ridden. Others are destination rides, worth making for their own sake: the Lindsey coast and the Skegness route are flat all the way and would suit older riders on e-bikes as much as anyone. The Trent Valley is both, but getting to work comes first.

That is a different aim from much of the National Cycle Network, which often seems to have been planned to complete a network on the map rather than to carry the most riders, winding between villages to find quiet lanes. Here the test is use: where the direct line runs beside traffic, the answer is to build a proper path there, not to send riders the long way round.

Nor is the model the Dutch town street, with its engineering standard for every junction. It is where the Danes, the Flemish and above all the Germans have built their long-distance paths: along rivers, canals and old railways from town to town. The Rhine cycle path follows the river from the Alps to the North Sea, and Germany has a path like it along most of its big rivers. Denmark has its national routes, Hærvejsruten among them, and Flanders has built cycle highways beside its canals and railway lines. England's flat river valleys and old railways could carry the same.

Choose a ride

Like Hærvejsløbet's set of routes, the Way splits into shorter rides, and the other routes bring Lincoln, Grimsby, Leicester, Leeds, Doncaster, Hull, Stamford, Teesside and the Lincolnshire coast into the network. Rides for getting to work come first, then destination rides. Each ride shows the towns of 10,000 people or more it links, and its total climbing, its highest point and its height profile, all on the same vertical scale so the hillier rides stand out.

Paths to build

Where the route has to use a road with traffic: A-road stretches of 300 m or more and B or C road stretches of 1 km or more, most predicted trips first, with the police-reported cycle collisions on each (2021–25). These are where a proper traffic-free path should be built, so nobody has to ride beside busy traffic.

Routes are proposals, routed with BRouter's touring profile through hand-picked villages on one bank of each river, so they follow the river without crossing back and forth or wandering off along signed routes between villages. They need riding and checking on the ground, especially farm tracks and towpaths after wet weather.
Riding conditions are measured every 25 m against OpenStreetMap: traffic-free is within 12 m of a cycleway or a path designated for bikes; quiet road a residential, unclassified or service road; track or lane no named road or cycle path within 12 m (unnamed lanes, farm tracks, towpaths); B or C road a secondary or tertiary road; A road a primary or trunk road. Collisions are police-reported injury collisions involving a bicycle within 30 m of the route (DfT STATS19, 2021–25). Climbing comes from BRouter's elevation data.
History is summarised from standard accounts of the Viking Age in England (the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle and recent archaeology at Repton, Heath Wood and Torksey). Place-name meanings are traditional derivations. Method and sources on GitHub.
