Where Dutch Infrastructure Should Work?

Hull and York are flat and among the highest-cycling places in England. Many towns here are just as flat, yet far fewer people cycle to work. Hills don't explain the gap; that points to roads, routes and how safe cycling feels.

Factors affecting cycling

Each mark is a town, shaped by region (circles East, triangles West, squares North, as below); bigger marks have more people travelling to work. Across: the average gradient of local commute routes. Up: the share of people travelling to work who cycle (Census 2021). The shaded band is as flat as Hull and York. The dashed curve is the best fit across all towns: cycling falls away steeply on the first gentle slopes and then levels off, roughly halving with every 1.5 points of gradient, rather than falling in a straight line. Towns well below the curve cycle less than their hills explain. Switch the colours to see each town's cycle collision rate against England and Wales: red where there are more collisions for each person who cycles to work, green where there are fewer.

Where the flat towns are

Every town shaded by how hilly its commute routes are, or for flat and gently sloping towns by the share of households with no car or how deprived its neighbourhoods are. Where few households have a car, a bike is often the cheapest way to reach work, and safe routes matter most. Deprivation against cycling to work

Each mark is a town, coloured by how hilly it is and shaped by region: circles in the East (Yorkshire, the East Midlands, Lincolnshire and Leicestershire), triangles in the West (Greater Manchester, Liverpool City Region, Lancashire, Cheshire and Warrington) and squares in the North (North East, Tees Valley and Cumbria). Bigger marks have more people travelling to work. Across: the share of its neighbourhoods in England's most deprived fifth (IMD 2025), or the share of households with no car (Census 2021). Up: the share of people travelling to work who cycle. The dashed line is the trend across the flat and gently sloping towns, leaving out Hull and York.

Towns are ONS built-up areas (2022) with at least 3,000 people travelling to work, built from their neighbourhoods (LSOAs) inside the CycleStats areas. Big cities appear as one built-up area; some suburbs and villages are their own.
Hilliness is the Propensity to Cycle Tool's average gradient of commute routes under 10 km from each neighbourhood, weighted by commuters. It measures the routes people would actually ride, not the landscape around a town.
Cycling is people who cycle to work as a share of people who travel to work (Census 2021, excluding those working mainly from home, which was high during the pandemic). The 2011 figure is the Census 2011 share of all commuters, from PCT, so the two are close but not identical measures.
No car is the share of households with no car or van (Census 2021), averaged over each town's neighbourhoods weighted by people travelling to work. Deprived is the share of its neighbourhoods in England's most deprived fifth on the Indices of Deprivation 2025 (a fifth by definition across England). Towns and networks are tagged Low car ownership where a quarter or more of households have no car, and Deprived where over a third of neighbourhoods are in the most deprived fifth.
Collisions vs England & Wales (colour the Factors affecting cycling chart by it, or hover a town) compares places, not risks to a rider. It is police-reported injury collisions involving a pedal cycle in the town's neighbourhoods (STATS19, 2021–25), a year, for every 100 people who cycle to work there (Census 2021), set against the same figure for England and Wales. The collisions include all cycling (shopping, school, leisure, visitors) while the Census counts only commuters, and many more people cycle than cycle to work, so it is not a cyclist's chance of a crash, which is far lower. A high figure can mean a lot of cycling that isn't commuting (Guisborough, on the edge of the North York Moors) as well as dangerous roads. Towns with fewer than 10 collisions are not compared. Serious is the share where someone was killed or seriously injured, and 40 mph+ the share on roads with a limit of 40 mph or more; these two do not depend on how many people cycle.
Missing cyclists is how many more people would cycle to work if the town matched Hull and York combined. Go Dutch is PCT's estimate of the share who would cycle with Dutch levels of cycling, given each town's trip lengths and hills. Method and sources on GitHub.

Where to build

The road corridors where well-engineered, protected cycle infrastructure would most cut cycle casualties and grow cycling. Each corridor is scored on the harm recorded there, how little of it is protected now, and how many more people would cycle on it under the Propensity to Cycle Tool's "Go Dutch" scenario. Each town above lists its own roads to improve.

Safety is the harm recorded on the stretch each year (police-reported cycle collisions 2021–25, with a killed or seriously injured casualty counted three times) multiplied by the share of the stretch without protection. Only a protected track counts as protection: a dedicated cycle track beside the road, or lanes separated from traffic by bollards, kerbs or wands. A painted lane, or a pavement shared with walkers or split from them by a painted line, counts as a quarter: these give little protection where side roads cross. OpenStreetMap often maps good kerb-separated tracks (such as parts of Leeds's Cycle Superhighways) the same way as poor pavement paths, so some good tracks are undercounted.
More cycling is the Propensity to Cycle Tool (PCT) "Go Dutch" estimate of commuters plus school pupils cycling along the stretch, minus those cycling there now. PCT models trips from the 2011 Census and the 2011 school census, so it shows relative potential, not a forecast. Its gender-equality scenario estimates commuting if women cycled as much as men.
Both ranks stretches with 5+ collisions that are at least half unprotected, by the geometric mean of the two scores (each scaled to the 90th percentile), so a road must score on both to rank highly.
Women and children are their share of the cyclist casualties within 30 m of the stretch (STATS19). Few women and children among casualties is consistent with few riding there, but casualty records cannot show who cycles.
Limits: protection comes from OpenStreetMap as mapped today, so schemes built during 2021–25 (for example the A660 Headingley Lane lanes in Leeds) will have collisions from before they opened. Junction design, which drives many collisions, is not assessed.

Cycling top 10s: all areas

The busiest counts, the roads with the most cycle collisions, the highest risk per rider and the safest well-used roads, ranked across every area. Select a row to open that area's map.

Most popular mixes Leeds City Council automatic counters (12-month averages) with DfT traffic counts, which are mostly single weekday counts on main roads. Some counters record one direction of a one-way lane, and a road can appear twice where it has two count points.
Collisions are police-reported injury collisions involving a bicycle (DfT STATS19, 2021–2025), matched to the nearest named road. A road is a connected stretch of one name, so long roads collect more.
Risk per rider = collisions ÷ (bikes per day × length × 365 × 5) × 1,000,000, for roads with 5+ collisions and a DfT cycle count. Where bike counts are low, a few collisions give a high rate, so treat the order as indicative.
Deprivation uses the English Indices of Deprivation 2025: the most deprived fifth means neighbourhoods (LSOAs) in deciles 1–2 for England as a whole. Collisions are placed in the neighbourhood STATS19 records for them. Each area map has a Neighbourhoods layer showing deprivation, housing condition and households with no car.
Safe & busy lists roads with 150+ bikes a day and the fewest collisions per km cycled. Off-road paths have no police collision data, so none appear here. Method and sources on GitHub.
