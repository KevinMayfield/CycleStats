Flat but Not Cycling

Hull and York are flat and among the highest-cycling places in England. Many towns here are just as flat, yet far fewer people cycle to work. Hills don't explain the gap; that points to roads, routes and how safe cycling feels.

Where the flat towns are

Every town shaded by how hilly its commute routes are, or for flat and gently sloping towns by the share of households with no car or how deprived its neighbourhoods are. Where few households have a car, a bike is often the cheapest way to reach work, and safe routes matter most. Dashed lines join flat and gently sloping towns whose built-up edges touch: together they make one network, where most short trips stay on level ground and cross from one town into the next. Factors affecting cycling

Each dot is a town; bigger dots have more people travelling to work. Across: the average gradient of local commute routes. Up: the share of people travelling to work who cycle (Census 2021). The shaded band is as flat as Hull and York. The dashed curve is the best fit across all towns: cycling falls away steeply on the first gentle slopes and then levels off, roughly halving with every 1.5 points of gradient, rather than falling in a straight line. Towns well below the curve cycle less than their hills explain. Switch the colours to see each town's cycle collision rate against England and Wales: red where there are more collisions for each person who cycles to work, green where there are fewer.

Deprivation against cycling to work

Each dot is a town, coloured by how hilly it is; bigger dots have more people travelling to work. Across: the share of its neighbourhoods in England's most deprived fifth (IMD 2025), or the share of households with no car (Census 2021). Up: the share of people travelling to work who cycle. The dashed line is the trend across the flat and gently sloping towns, leaving out Hull and York.

Towns are ONS built-up areas (2022) with at least 3,000 people travelling to work, built from their neighbourhoods (LSOAs) inside the CycleStats areas. Big cities appear as one built-up area; some suburbs and villages are their own.
Hilliness is the Propensity to Cycle Tool's average gradient of commute routes under 10 km from each neighbourhood, weighted by commuters. It measures the routes people would actually ride, not the landscape around a town.
Cycling is people who cycle to work as a share of people who travel to work (Census 2021, excluding those working mainly from home, which was high during the pandemic). The 2011 figure is the Census 2011 share of all commuters, from PCT, so the two are close but not identical measures.
No car is the share of households with no car or van (Census 2021), averaged over each town's neighbourhoods weighted by people travelling to work. Deprived is the share of its neighbourhoods in England's most deprived fifth on the Indices of Deprivation 2025 (a fifth by definition across England). Towns and networks are tagged Low car ownership where a quarter or more of households have no car, and Deprived where over a third of neighbourhoods are in the most deprived fifth.
Collisions vs England & Wales (colour the Factors affecting cycling chart by it, or hover a town) compares places, not risks to a rider. It is police-reported injury collisions involving a pedal cycle in the town's neighbourhoods (STATS19, 2021–25), a year, for every 100 people who cycle to work there (Census 2021), set against the same figure for England and Wales. The collisions include all cycling (shopping, school, leisure, visitors) while the Census counts only commuters, and many more people cycle than cycle to work, so it is not a cyclist's chance of a crash, which is far lower. A high figure can mean a lot of cycling that isn't commuting (Guisborough, on the edge of the North York Moors) as well as dangerous roads. Towns with fewer than 10 collisions are not compared. Serious is the share where someone was killed or seriously injured, and 40 mph+ the share on roads with a limit of 40 mph or more; these two do not depend on how many people cycle.
Missing cyclists is how many more people would cycle to work if the town matched Hull and York combined. Go Dutch is PCT's estimate of the share who would cycle with Dutch levels of cycling, given each town's trip lengths and hills. Method and sources on GitHub.
