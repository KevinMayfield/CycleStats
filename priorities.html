Where to Build

The road corridors where well-engineered, protected cycle infrastructure would most cut cycle casualties and grow cycling. Each corridor is scored on the harm recorded there, how little of it is protected now, and how many more people would cycle on it under the Propensity to Cycle Tool's "Go Dutch" scenario.

Safety is the harm recorded on the stretch each year (police-reported cycle collisions 2021–25, with a killed or seriously injured casualty counted three times) multiplied by the share of the stretch without protection. A protected track scores as full protection; a painted lane or a shared-use pavement counts as half.
More cycling is the Propensity to Cycle Tool (PCT) "Go Dutch" estimate of commuters plus school pupils cycling along the stretch, minus those cycling there now. PCT models trips from the 2011 Census and the 2011 school census, so it shows relative potential, not a forecast. Its gender-equality scenario estimates commuting if women cycled as much as men.
Both ranks stretches with 5+ collisions that are at least half unprotected, by the geometric mean of the two scores (each scaled to the 90th percentile), so a road must score on both to rank highly.
Women and children are their share of the cyclist casualties within 30 m of the stretch (STATS19). Few women and children among casualties is consistent with few riding there, but casualty records cannot show who cycles.
Limits: protection comes from OpenStreetMap as mapped today, so schemes built during 2021–25 (for example the A660 Headingley Lane lanes in Leeds) will have collisions from before they opened. Junction design, which drives many collisions, is not assessed. Method and sources on GitHub.
