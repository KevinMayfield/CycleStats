# CycleStats: what the data says about cycling across Yorkshire and the East Midlands

*Summary written 27 October 2026. Live site: <https://kevinmayfield.github.io/CycleStats/>*

CycleStats maps cycling usage, safety and potential for 18 areas: all of Yorkshire, Teesside, Lincolnshire,
Nottinghamshire, Derbyshire, Leicestershire and Rutland. It began as a map of Leeds' busiest and most dangerous cycle
routes. It grew into a set of pages that ask one question: **where would proper cycling infrastructure actually be
used?**

## Key findings

### 1. More people cycle where the land is flat

Across 194 towns of about 10,000 people or more, cycling to work falls steadily as the terrain gets hillier:

| Terrain (average gradient of commutes under 10 km) | Towns | Cycle to work |
|---|---|---|
| Hull and York (the benchmarks) | 2 | 9.3% |
| Flat (no hillier than Hull and York, plus a margin) | 54 | 4.4% |
| Gentle slopes (up to 2%) | 39 | 3.1% |
| Hilly | 99 | 1.7% |

The correlation between gradient and cycling to work is strong (−0.71 on a log scale). Cycling to work roughly halves
with every extra 1.5% of gradient.

That raises a question for policy. "Cycling infrastructure like the Netherlands" should start in the places whose
terrain is like the Netherlands. The same goes for Flanders, the German river valleys along the Rhine and Denmark,
which are the other places where long-distance and everyday cycling paths are most used. England has plenty of flat
river valleys and towns. Those are where Dutch-style results are most likely.

### 2. More people cycle where fewer collisions occur

Towns with more cycling have fewer collisions per person cycling. The correlation between a town's cycling share
and its collision rate is −0.5 on a log scale. Collisions are measured per 100 cycle commuters a year, against the
England & Wales rate of 2.7 (STATS19, 2021–25):

- **Flat towns:** a median 1.4 collisions a year per 100 cycle commuters, about half the national rate.
- **Hilly towns:** 3.1, above the national rate.
- **York:** 1.8 (about a third below England & Wales), with the highest cycling share of any town in the region at
  12.5%.

Cause and effect run both ways. Safer roads attract riders, and more riders make drivers expect them. Either way,
safety and use go together, and infrastructure has to deliver both.

### 3. Painted cycle lanes are not making roads noticeably safer

Across 3,131 measured road stretches, collisions per million kilometres cycled (from DfT cycle counts) come out as:

| Stretch | Stretches with counts | Collisions per million bike-km |
|---|---|---|
| No cycle provision | 1,656 | 4.2 |
| Painted lane (half or more of the stretch) | 247 | 3.9 |
| Physically protected (half or more) | 15 | 2.7 |

Painted lanes are barely safer than nothing: 7% fewer collisions per kilometre ridden. The few properly protected
stretches are about a third lower, although 15 stretches is a small sample. Derby Road in Long Eaton is a local
example. It has a painted lane on a narrow footway with many side-road crossings, and a high collision count. Many
painted-lane roads like it are the obvious candidates for proper Dutch-style segregated paths.

### 4. York and Hull stand out, and Hull most of all

York (12.5%) and Hull (8.0%) cycle far more than any other large place in the region. They are the benchmarks the
other towns are measured against.

Hull especially would gain from segregated paths:

- **Collisions:** 965 cycle collisions in five years, second only to Leeds, and 224 killed or seriously injured.
- **Where to build:** 7 of the 10 road stretches in the site's combined safety-and-use ranking are in Hull. They
  include Spring Bank West, Princes Avenue, Spring Bank, Anlaby Road, Holderness Road, Beverley Road and James
  Reckitt Avenue.
- **Potential:** it has the highest Go Dutch potential of any town in the region, 38.6% cycling to work.
- **Build these first:** Hull to Beverley is the top-ranked stage of the Danelaw Cycle Network. 13,037 people commute
  between Hull, Cottingham and Beverley (Census 2021), and about 910 trips a day are predicted on the 6 km that need a
  path.

Hull already cycles because it is flat. Protected routes would make it safer and could take it towards the Dutch
levels its terrain allows.

### 5. Derby–Nottingham, Middlesbrough and Derbyshire are falling behind

- **Middlesbrough** is as flat as York (gradient 1.1%) but only 2.4% cycle to work. That is below even what its terrain
  predicts (3.9%), against a Go Dutch potential of 28%. It is the clearest flat underperformer in the region.
- **The Derby–Nottingham corridor** is weak for its terrain:
  - Derby 3.2%, Nottingham 3.7% and Long Eaton 4.3%, against Go Dutch potentials of 22–26%.
  - Long Eaton is flat (gradient 0.6%) but cycles at less than half the rate of Goole (10.8%), a smaller town on
    similar ground.
  - Beeston (6.3%) shows what the corridor can do.
- **Derby to Nottingham by Long Eaton could reach high cycling numbers.**
  - **Population:** the towns along it (Derby, Long Eaton, Sandiacre, Stapleford, Beeston, Nottingham and West
    Bridgford) have about 226,000 people travelling to work.
  - **Terrain:** the line between them follows the Derwent and the Trent. It climbs only 6 m from Derby to Long
    Eaton.
  - **Potential:** 3.8% cycle to work today (about 8,400 people). The Go Dutch scenario puts the corridor at 23%,
    about 51,000.
  - **Why the route prediction is low:** two things hold it down.
    - Risk of injury: Derby had 397 cycle collisions and Nottingham 581 in five years, and 31% of Long Eaton's cycle
      collisions killed or seriously injured someone. That keeps today's numbers low.
    - Missing data: the Propensity to Cycle Tool predicts flows on roads, and the river paths run well away from
      them. Between Derby and Newark, 70 of 154 half-kilometre stretches get under 25 predicted trips a day, and 30
      get none.
  - **Taking both into account:** the corridor is probably one of the biggest opportunities in the region, not one
    of the smallest.
  - **Census commuting confirms it:** 7,171 people commute between Beeston and Nottingham, the biggest flow between
    any two towns in the region. Another 2,341 commute between Long Eaton and Nottingham and 2,240 between Derby and
    Long Eaton. On those figures, Long Eaton to Nottingham ranks 3rd and Derby to Long Eaton 7th among the network's
    flat stages.
- **Derby and Derbyshire have a safety problem:**
  - Half of Derbyshire's towns (8 of 16) have collision rates above England & Wales.
  - Its neighbours do better: Nottinghamshire is 5% below the national rate, Lincolnshire 38% below and
    Leicestershire 49% below.
  - In Long Eaton, 31% of cycle collisions kill or seriously injure.
  - London Road in Derby is in the site's top 10 places to build.
  - Derbyshire also has one of the widest gaps between where collisions happen and where people live: 35% of its
    cycle collisions are in the most deprived fifth of neighbourhoods, where 20% of residents live.

West Yorkshire has higher collision rates still: Bradford is 98% above England & Wales, Kirklees and Calderdale 68%
and Leeds 39%. But those areas are hilly. Derbyshire stands out among the gentler East Midlands.

### 6. Towns 5–12 km apart are where cycling to work can grow

The Census 2021 home-to-work flows show where people actually travel. The biggest flows between towns are almost all
between neighbours 4–12 km apart. That distance is too far to walk but an easy cycle, especially by e-bike:

| Towns | Distance | People commuting between them |
|---|---|---|
| Beeston ↔ Nottingham | 6.7 km | 7,171 |
| Cottingham ↔ Hull | 5.0 km | 6,611 |
| Beverley ↔ Hull | 10.3 km | 5,470 |
| Hessle ↔ Hull | 7.5 km | 4,657 |
| Long Eaton ↔ Nottingham | 11.4 km | 2,341 |
| Derby ↔ Long Eaton | 12.7 km | 2,215 |

Beyond about 15 km the flows fall away: 1,701 people commute between Leeds and York (34 km), and 403 between Hull and
York (55 km). Long routes between cities are destination rides. The everyday value of a route lies in the short links
between neighbouring towns along it. So the network's "Build these first" ranking counts the people commuting between
towns within 15 km of each other along each stage.

### 7. Build paths people will use

"Cyclists don't use cycle paths" is too often true today. Paths tend to be built to meet a quota, to complete a
network on the map, or at the request of enthusiast groups, which are often male dominated. Only 17% of cycling
casualties on the measured roads are women, which shows how narrow today's cycling population is.

The approach here is different. Build first where the land is flat, where people already make short trips to work
and school, and where a path is missing on the direct line. Paths built on those terms carry people from the day
they open. That answers the complaint directly, and it gives every council a success story to point to before
moving on to towns on gentle slopes.

### 8. Collisions follow shops and side roads, not traffic

*Added 27 October 2026, across the 15 areas including the North West and North East.*

What do the safest busy roads have in common, and what do the roads with the most collisions share? The summary's
top 10s were extended to the top 100 of each:

- **Safe & busy:** 150+ bikes a day and the lowest collision rate.
- **Most collisions:** ranked by collision count.
- **Busy, highest rate:** a third group, 150+ bikes a day ranked by collisions per bike-km, so it isn't swayed by
  length.

Each road was then measured against OpenStreetMap:

- shops, cafés, pubs and takeaways within 40 m;
- side roads joining it;
- bus stops and traffic signals;
- cycle paths running alongside;
- its road class and speed limit.

| Median per road | Safe & busy | Most collisions | Busy, highest rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Length | 1.2 km | 2.9 km | 1.4 km |
| Shops and cafés per km | 0.4 | 1.9 | 3.0 |
| Side roads per km | 2.4 | 2.0 | 3.3 |
| Cycle path alongside | 17% of length | 2% | 3% |
| Shared pavement (mean) | 30% | 10% | 17% |
| 40 mph or more (mean share) | 36% | 10% | 9% |
| Deprivation decile (1 = most deprived) | 5 | 3 | 3.5 |
| Households with no car | 27% | 40% | 39% |

The safest busy roads are **distributor roads**: inner ring roads, dock roads and bypasses like Tower Road
(Birkenhead docks), Trafford Road (Salford Quays), Sheaf Street (Sheffield) and Frederick Ward Way (Grimsby). They have
few shops, often a path alongside so people cycle off the carriageway, and faster limits. The roads with the most
collisions are **radial high streets**: Wilmslow Road (Rusholme), Otley Road and Headingley Lane, Smithdown Road, and
Hull's Anlaby, Beverley and Holderness Roads. They are lined with shops and bus stops and run through deprived,
car-free neighbourhoods. They top the list partly because they are long and busy: their rate per bike-km is close to
average.

Top-100 lists pick out extremes, so the pattern was checked across every busy counted stretch (452 stretches, 1,832
collisions, 2021–25). Rates are pooled cycle collisions per million bike-km (95% bootstrap interval):

| Along the road | Stretches | Collisions per million bike-km | Killed or seriously injured |
|---|---|---|---|
| All busy counted stretches | 452 | 3.5 | 0.91 |
| Shops and cafés: under 0.5 per km | 163 | 2.1 | 0.58 |
| 0.5–2 per km | 126 | 3.5 | 0.87 |
| 2–6 per km | 107 | 4.8 | 1.25 |
| 6 or more per km | 56 | 6.6 (5.4–8.1) | 1.79 |
| Side roads: under 1.5 per km | 138 | 2.7 | 0.70 |
| 6 or more per km | 48 | 6.3 (4.7–8.2) | 1.43 |
| Bus stops: under 0.5 per km | 97 | 2.6 | 0.72 |
| 3 or more per km | 39 | 6.2 (4.5–8.1) | 1.67 |
| Distributor: under 0.5 shops and under 3 side roads per km | 125 | 1.9 | 0.53 |
| High street: 2+ shops and 3+ side roads per km | 101 | 5.7 | 1.39 |

- **Frontage matters most.** Each step up in shops, side roads and bus stops raises the rate. A high street has about
  three times the collisions per kilometre cycled of a distributor road, and about two and a half times the serious
  ones. The same holds on 30 mph roads alone: 2.8 with almost no shops against 7.1 with six or more per km. So it is
  not just the speed limit.
- **Traffic volume makes little difference.** Rates are about the same at 150 bikes a day and at 700+, so busier
  cycling roads are not more dangerous per rider.
- **A path alongside helps on distributor roads (1.7 with one, 2.3 without), but not on high streets (5.6 against
  5.8).** On a high street, people on bikes still cross every side road, shop entrance and bus stop. This fits finding
  3: paint and partial protection change little where the conflicts are at junctions and the kerbside.
- **Higher limits look safer (0.8 per million bike-km at 50 mph+), but that is misleading.** These are bypasses where
  most people ride on a path beside the road, while the DfT count is taken on the carriageway.
- **Deprived and car-free neighbourhoods have higher rates:** 4.3 in deciles 1–2 against 2.8 in deciles 8–10, and 4.2
  where 40%+ of households have no car against 2.7 under 20%. Much of this overlaps with the high streets running
  through them.

**Chance plays a big part in the safe list.** At the average rate, a typical safe-list stretch would expect only 1.6
collisions in five years. Across the 452 stretches, about 63 would have none by luck alone, against 91 that do. So
any one road with zero collisions is not proven safe. What holds up is the pattern across all of them.

**What this means for building.** The hard places are the radial high streets, where cycling demand, deprivation and
collisions meet. A path alongside a distributor road is cheap and works. On a high street it needs Dutch-style
protection that carries through junctions:

- continuous footways across side roads;
- protected junctions;
- bus stop bypasses;
- loading moved off the cycle track.

Limits:
- OpenStreetMap coverage of shops and side roads varies by area.
- DfT counts are often single-day or estimated.
- Collisions snap to the nearest named road.
- Shops are counted within 40 m of the road's centre line.

The analysis scripts are not in the pipeline yet.

### 9. Coalfield and steel towns: work is further away, but that is only part of it

*Added 28 October 2026. See the [Industry and work page](https://kevinmayfield.github.io/CycleStats/industry.html).*

Coalfield towns cycle about 24% less than similar towns elsewhere, and the iron, steel and shipbuilding towns about
27% less. "Similar" means the same hills, deprivation, car ownership, size, region and surrounding conurbation. Three
explanations were tested, each by adding it to that model:

1. **The pits and works closed, so new work is too far to cycle to.**
2. **Nationalisation and rehousing moved people onto new estates and broke an older cycling habit.**
3. **The railway towns, where the railway age brought new settlers.**

| Flat and gently sloping towns | Towns | Commutes under 5 km | Commutes 10 km or more | Homes built 1945–82, band A or B | Cyclists per 100 people with a commute under 5 km |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Coalfield | 50 | 43.8% | 33.6% | 31.5% | 7.0 |
| Iron, steel and shipbuilding | 13 | 53.3% | 26.4% | 31.3% | 6.2 |
| Other | 186 | 50.2% | 29.1% | 22.3% | 8.9 |

- **Distance explains about a fifth of the coalfield gap.** Coalfield towns have fewer short commutes and more long
  ones. The share of commutes under 5 km is the strongest new factor: every 10 points more goes with 18% more cycling.
  Allowing for it takes the coalfield gap from −24% to −20%.
- **Distance does not explain the steel towns.** Their work is as close as anywhere (53% of commutes under 5 km), yet
  they still cycle about 27% less.
- **Short trips still go by other means.** Counting only people whose work is under 5 km away, coalfield towns have 7.0
  cyclists per 100 and steel towns 6.2, against 8.9 elsewhere. Most of the gap is in how short journeys are made, not
  where the work went.
- **The post-war estates are there, but make no difference.** Coalfield and steel towns have more cheap (band A or B)
  homes built 1945–82, mostly council estates: about 31% of homes against 22% elsewhere. With everything else held
  equal, towns with more of them cycle no less (−1% per 10 points, likely −4% to +2%). Nor do the shares of homes built
  before 1919 or since 1983, or of social renting today. The moves may still have broken older habits; housing age
  cannot show that.
- **Railway towns follow the coalfield, not the railway** (+8% overall, likely −10% to +27%). The big railway works
  towns on flat land cycle more than their hills predict: Crewe +61%, March +36%, Peterborough +27% and Derby +23%. York,
  the highest-cycling town, is a railway town too. Those on the coalfields cycle less: Doncaster −17%, Shildon −35% and
  Newton-le-Willows −48%.
- **Irish settlement can't be tested.** The Census does not record ancestry, and people born in Ireland today are mostly
  recent arrivals.

What is left is likely the culture and conditions of short local journeys: works and colliery buses, then the car;
shift patterns; poorer health; and main roads through former pit villages. These are not measured here.

Sources:
- Census 2021 TS058, distance travelled to work.
- Valuation Office Agency, Council Tax stock of properties 2024, table CTSOP 4.1: homes by build period and band, by
  neighbourhood.
- The page's cycling model, in `pipeline/industry.py`.

### 10. Women ride where cycling is normal, not where the roads look safer

*Added 29 October 2026. See [Who cycles](https://kevinmayfield.github.io/CycleStats/people.html).*

Could fear keep women off bikes, including fear fed by media coverage that paints cycling as dangerous or
confrontational? Recorded danger was tested first: collisions per cyclist have only a weak link with women's share of
riders. Media works on *felt* danger, though: busy traffic, speed and the lack of protection, which collision records
barely show.

So every measured main road was placed in its town and summarised by:
- the share with a protected track;
- the share at 40 mph or more;
- the share that is busy (15,000+ vehicles a day) and unprotected.

Each road is weighted by the commuting the Propensity to Cycle Tool's Go Dutch scenario puts on it: the roads people
would need to use. Across 192 towns, holding cycling level, hills, car ownership and the coalfield equal:

| Compared with women's share of cycle commuters | Correlation | Effect | Likely range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Collisions per cyclist (50 index points more) | −0.07 | +3% | −2% to +9% |
| Main roads protected (10 points more) | −0.02 | −3% | −8% to +5% |
| Main roads busy and unprotected (10 points more) | +0.01 | 0% | −1% to +1% |
| Main roads at 40 mph or more (10 points more) | −0.01 | 0% | −2% to +2% |
| **How many cycle to work** | **+0.71** | strongest link | |

By cycling level (median, the 193 towns with a women's share and measured roads, including one without a collision index):

| Cycle to work | Towns | Women's share of riders |
|---|---|---|
| Under 2% | 92 | 14% |
| 2–4% | 67 | 22% |
| 4–7% | 27 | 30% |
| 7% or more | 7 | 37% |

- **Women's share follows how normal cycling is**, and the hills (−9% for each extra point of route gradient). It does
  not follow recorded danger or any road measure.
- **That does not show the roads don't matter.** The typical town has none of its main commuting routes protected, and
  only 16 of 192 have protection on even a tenth of them. The most protected, Sutton-in-Ashfield (47%), has few women
  riding (13%), but one town proves nothing. Every English town is in effect a "busy, unprotected main roads" town, so
  comparing towns cannot show what protection would do.
- **What fits is fear and image working through how common cycling is.** Coverage that frames cycling as dangerous or
  confrontational would keep cycling to the few, and where few cycle, few of them are women. These figures can't prove
  it. The test is before and after: how women's share changes where a town builds protected routes. The next Census
  that could show this is in 2031.

Calculated in `pipeline/people.py` (`roads`) from the area maps' measured road stretches, DfT traffic counts, the
Propensity to Cycle Tool and Census 2021 travel to work by sex.

## The Danelaw Cycle Network

The [Danelaw Cycle Network](https://kevinmayfield.github.io/CycleStats/danelaw.html) brings the findings together as
a proposed network of flat, safe routes between towns. It runs along the rivers, old roads and railways of the part of
England once under Danish rule, and along the Roman roads before them.

- **Size:** 14 routes and about 1,710 km, linking 59 towns where 1.3 million people travel to work.
- **The spine:** the Danelaw Way, Repton to Stamford Bridge (300 km, 287 m of climbing).
- **Getting-to-work routes** keep to the direct line with no scenic detours: Trent Valley, Soar, Aire (Leeds to
  Hull), Don (Sheffield to Goole), Beverley (Hull to York), Tadcaster (Leeds to York), Barton to Hull over the Humber
  Bridge, Hull to Bridlington, and York to Middlesbrough.
- **Destination rides** are flat enough for a much wider set of people, older riders on e-bikes among them:
  - the Lincolnshire coast
  - the Skegness route
  - Holland and the Wash
  - the Lindsey Ridings loop
  - York to Bridlington over the Wolds
  - the Great North Road, Stamford to Middlesbrough
- **Build these first:** a ranking by the people who commute between the towns each stage links (Census 2021, towns
  within 15 km), flat routes first, with the predicted trips a day shown alongside. The top flat stages are:
  1. Hull to Beverley: 13,037 commuters
  2. Cleethorpes to Barton-upon-Humber: 11,954
  3. Long Eaton to Nottingham: 11,851
  4. Doncaster to Thorne: 7,838
  5. Hessle to Hull: 6,847

  Among routes with some climbing, Sheffield to Rotherham and Mexborough leads, with 14,936.
- **History:** the routes pass 31 Danelaw sites and 17 Roman sites, from the Great Army's camp at Repton to
  Eboracum and the battlefield at Stamford Bridge.

### Why the Danes' and Romans' routes

Long-distance routes are most likely to work on the lines the Danes and the Romans used. The Danes travelled by river,
up the Trent, the Ouse, the Humber and their tributaries. The Romans built direct roads between their forts and towns:
Ermine Street, the Fosse Way and the road north to York. Both chose the flat valleys and the direct lines, and most of
the region's towns grew up along them. Riding those corridors today means:

- **Flat ground:** river valleys and the old roads avoid the hills.
- **Towns in a line:** a route along them links one town to the next, so it serves everyday trips as well as long
  rides.
- **A ready-made story:** the history gives a long-distance route a reason to exist and something to ride for, as the
  Hærvej, Denmark's old army road, does for Hærvejsløbet.

The model is not the Dutch town street with its engineering standard for every junction. It is the long-distance
paths of Denmark, Flanders and above all Germany, which follow rivers, canals and old railways from town to town, with
a proper path built wherever the direct line meets traffic.

## How this was built

The site is a static GitHub Pages app (Leaflet maps) fed by a Python pipeline, `pipeline/run_all.sh`. Its sources are:

- OpenStreetMap
- DfT STATS19 collisions (2021–25) and traffic counts
- council bike counters
- Census 2021 travel to work and car ownership
- the English Indices of Deprivation 2025
- the Propensity to Cycle Tool (Go Dutch scenario)
- BRouter for routing

It was built in conversation with Claude Code over a series of pull requests. In order:

1. Leeds maps and usage
2. regional areas
3. deprivation layers
4. Where to build
5. flat towns, renamed "Where Dutch infrastructure should work"
6. collision RAG ratings against England & Wales
7. per-town road suggestions
8. the Danelaw network and its routes
9. predicted cycling and the build-first ranking
10. the Great North Road and Roman sites

## Limits

- **Census coverage:** the Census measures travel to work only, and its 2021 figures fell during the pandemic.
- **PCT coverage:** the Propensity to Cycle Tool covers commuting and school trips only, so leisure routes are
  undervalued.
- **Collisions:** only police-reported collisions are counted. Off-road incidents and unreported crashes are missing.
- **Collision rates:** these are per cycle commuter, a rough guide to exposure. The painted-lane comparison uses only
  stretches with DfT cycle counts, and the protected group is small.
- **Cause and effect:** the figures are associations between towns and roads. They do not prove what causes what.
