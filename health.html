Health and Active Travel

Where would more cycling do most for health? This page compares the flat and gently sloping towns, where cycling is most likely to take off, on health problems linked to too little exercise: inactive adults, overweight and obesity, diabetes, obese children and poor health. It then sets that against how many people cycle to work there now and how many could. The league table puts first the towns where health is worst and most of the potential for cycling is unused.

Health need against cycling to work

Each dot is a town of about 10,000 people or more, sized by the people who travel to work there. Up is less healthy than England (100 = England's level; the index is explained below); right is more cycling to work (Census 2021). The towns that matter most here are high and to the left: unhealthy and hardly cycling, but flat enough to change. Hover or tap a dot for its figures.

Where active travel should improve health most

Flat and gently sloping towns, with Hull and York, ranked by a health gain score: the health need index (as a share of England's) times the gap between cycling to work today and the Propensity to Cycle Tool's Go Dutch scenario. Coloured figures are against England: red worse by 10% or more, amber within 10%, green better. Deaths avoided and their value are the tool's own estimates (WHO HEAT method) for commuting alone, if the town reached Go Dutch.

What more cycling is worth to each NHS area

The Propensity to Cycle Tool's health estimates summed over every neighbourhood in each NHS integrated care board (ICB), for cycling to work alone. "Today" is the value of the cycling to work that already happens. "Go Dutch" is the extra value each year if commuting reached Dutch levels, split into lives saved and sick days avoided; "E-bikes" is the tool's scenario where e-bikes extend how far people will ride. These are the WHO Health Economic Assessment Tool's values for health to society, not savings in NHS budgets: see the note under the table.
