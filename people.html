Who Cycles

Where few people cycle, it is mostly men, and mostly younger men in the hilly cities. As cycling grows, women catch up: in the towns that cycle most, close to half of cycle commuters are women, as in the Netherlands. In the flat towns that stand on their own, people in their sixties and older still at work cycle almost as much as people in their twenties. The things that hold cycling back (hills, conurbations, the coalfields) hold back women and older riders most.

Towns with 8,000 or more people travelling to work. Click a column heading to sort by it. Towns where ONS withholds the split by sex are listed without it. 50–64s vs 16–34s is how likely 50–64s are to cycle to work against 16–34s: above 1 means a town's cycling rests more on older riders. Cycle collisions are police-recorded collisions involving a bicycle in the town, 2021–25, a year per 10,000 residents, with how many killed or seriously injured someone; the typical town has . Towns where many people cycle have more collisions per head simply because more people are on bikes. The last column sets the same collisions against an estimate of the adults who cycle at least once a week, for any reason: the town's cycle commuters (Census 2021) scaled up by how many weekly cyclists its council has for each cycle commuter (Sport England's Active Lives survey, 2019, 2022 and 2023). It is an index, with the typical town at 100, so 200 means twice the collisions for the cycling done there. It compares towns only: it is not anyone's chance of a crash, since the collisions include children and every kind of trip, and the estimate rests on council-wide survey figures.
